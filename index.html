Understanding earthquakes

An earthquake is a natural phenomenon in which the ground shakes due to the sudden release of energy within the Earth.

Earth has four main layers: the crust, mantle, outer core, and inner core. The crust and the rigid uppermost part of the mantle together form the lithosphere, which is divided into large sections called tectonic plates.

These plates move slowly over geological time due to processes within the mantle. Their movement creates stress along faults (fractures in Earth’s crust). When the accumulated stress exceeds the strength of the rocks, they suddenly slip, releasing energy in the form of seismic waves. These waves travel through the Earth and cause the ground shaking we experience as an earthquake.1

Why analysing earthquakes matters

Earthquakes vary in magnitude, and larger earthquakes can have devastating consequences, including loss of life and widespread destruction. Early reports of the Nagqu earthquake counted at least 16 deaths.7 Understanding tectonic activity and monitoring earthquake-related changes are essential for assessing hazards and reducing their impacts.

Earthquakes can move the Earth’s surface, sometimes by just a few centimeters. Observing these changes across large and remote regions with ground-based instruments alone is challenging. This is where satellite technology becomes valuable.

How NISAR works

NISAR (NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar) is an Earth-observing satellite mission that uses active remote sensing to study changes on Earth’s surface. Unlike optical satellites that rely on sunlight reflected from the ground, NISAR generates its own microwave signals and sends them toward Earth. A portion of the energy is reflected back to the satellite, where it is recorded.

NISAR uses Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), which combines radar measurements collected along the satellite’s flight path to create high-resolution images. Because radar works in the microwave part of the spectrum, it can observe during both day and night and through clouds, which makes it useful for remote regions and natural disasters. The returned signals carry information about surface roughness, structure, geometry, and moisture.2

For earthquakes, scientists use Interferometric SAR (InSAR). By comparing the phase of radar signals from two acquisitions, they can detect subtle ground displacement, potentially down to centimeter-scale changes under suitable conditions. NISAR cannot predict when an earthquake will occur, but it shows how Earth’s surface responds to one.

Study area and data

We selected a region of the Tibetan Plateau covering 31.88°–32.88° N and 93.28°–94.28° E. It was chosen after the magnitude 7.0 earthquake of 22 October 2026, which lies near its center at a depth of about 18 km.4 The region is tectonically active, so the event is a good case for studying earthquake-related surface deformation with NISAR.

We searched ASF Vertex for NISAR data covering the study area around the earthquake date. Several Geocoded Unwrapped Interferogram (GUNW) files were available. We selected the pair whose acquisitions fall on 19 and 31 October 2026. It spans the earthquake: the first acquisition is about 2.7 days before it and the second about 9.4 days after.5

How to read it

The satellite measures only movement along its line of sight, the direction from the ground to the radar. Fringes are the colored bands: the tighter they are packed, the faster the ground changed over a short distance.

What it shows

Dense fringes wrap around the epicentre, and the ground on the two sides of it moved in opposite directions relative to the satellite. Far from the fault the picture is quiet, with residual noise of about 1.5 cm. The small blank area near the epicentre has no usable phase.

How we made it

We masked pixels with coherence below 0.3 or failed unwrapping, removed a smooth trend fitted to areas more than 100 km from the epicentre, and converted phase to displacement using the 24 cm wavelength. The blank diagonal strip is a gap in the product.

Limitations

This is a student analysis of a rapid-response product. It is not an official deformation model and should not be used for emergency decisions.

  • One ascending pair gives one viewing direction. Vertical and east-west motion cannot be separated.
  • The 12-day pair includes aftershocks and about nine days of later movement, not only the mainshock.
  • The ionosphere estimate in the product showed a sharp feature at the epicentre, so we did not apply it. Troposphere corrections were not applied either.
  • Movement very close to the fault is too large for the color scales, and the radar signal is lost in places.
  • We have not compared the result with ground measurements or published models.

Next steps: add a descending pass, apply atmospheric corrections, and track pixel offsets near the fault.
